Think of a small factory that makes custom metal parts, like brackets, gears or housings, for other companies.

How it works today

A customer emails: “Can you make 500 of this part? Here’s the drawing. What’s your price and delivery time?” That email is called an RFQ (request for quotation).
An engineer at the factory opens the drawing and reads it by hand: what material, what size, how many holes, how precise it needs to be.
The engineer then works out the cost: how much raw material, which machines, how many hours and which tools.
To do that, they dig through old spreadsheets and the ERP (the company’s main business software) to find similar jobs and what was charged before.
Finally they type up a price and send it back.

The problem

Each quote takes about 2.5 hours of an engineer’s time, and a factory gets dozens of these every week.
The information is scattered across 5–7 places (email, drawings, ERP, spreadsheets), so the reply takes 3–5 days.
The customer sent the same request to several factories, so a slow reply often means a faster competitor gets the order.
Most quotes don’t turn into orders anyway, so skilled engineers spend much of their week on paperwork that earns nothing.
The pricing know-how often lives in one senior person’s head, and when they’re busy or on leave, quoting stops.

In short, the factory loses orders because pricing a job is slow, manual work, even though it is perfectly able to make the part.

What the AI fix does

It reads the drawing and pulls out the material, dimensions and tolerances.
It searches the ERP for similar parts made before and what they cost.
It drafts a quote in minutes.
The engineer only checks and approves it, instead of building it from scratch.

So a 3–5 day reply becomes a same-day reply, with the same team and the same old ERP still in place.
